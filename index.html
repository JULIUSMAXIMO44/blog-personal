Critical Thinking & Media Literacy

Do You Really Believe Everything You Read Online?

In a world flooded with information, the ability to think critically and read media with a questioning mind is no longer optional — it is essential for every citizen.

JC
ADSO JULIO CÉSAR RUDAS SANCHEZ · September 2026 · 6 min read

What is Media Literacy?

Media literacy is the ability to access, analyze, evaluate, and create media in various forms. It means understanding that every piece of content — a news article, a social media post, a documentary — was created by someone with a specific point of view, a purpose, and sometimes an agenda.

In the digital age, the volume of information we receive every day is overwhelming. According to researchers, the average person is exposed to thousands of pieces of content daily. The critical question is not how much we consume — but how well we filter it.

"Not everything that is shared is true, and not everything that is true is shared. The reader's job is to find out which is which."

A Personal Experience with Fake News

Last month, I came across a viral post on Instagram claiming that a famous Colombian scientist had discovered a cure for diabetes. The post had thousands of likes and hundreds of comments saying "God bless this man." Out of curiosity — and applying what I learned in my SENA formation — I decided to investigate:

  • I searched for the scientist's name on Google Scholar — no results whatsoever.
  • I checked three reliable news sources: El Tiempo, BBC Mundo, Reuters — none mentioned the story.
  • I used a reverse image search — the photo was taken from a 2018 unrelated medical conference.
  • I found the original post — it had been shared from a page known for health misinformation.

The post was completely false. But thousands of people believed it, shared it, and commented on it. This experience made me understand why media literacy is not a luxury — it is a survival skill in the information age.

Critical Thinking: Asking Better Questions

Critical thinking is not about being negative or cynical. It is about being systematic. It means applying a consistent set of questions before accepting or sharing information:

  • Who created this? Is there an author, an institution, a clear source?
  • What is the purpose? To inform, persuade, entertain, or sell?
  • What evidence supports it? Are there statistics, studies, experts cited?
  • What perspectives are missing? Whose voice is not in this story?
  • How does this make me feel? Emotional content is often designed to override reason.

My Checklist for Spotting Fake News

  • Check the source: Is it a recognized institution or media outlet with editorial standards?
  • Read beyond the headline: Headlines are often misleading or incomplete.
  • Look for dates: Old news is frequently reposted as if it were current.
  • Cross-check with other sources: If only one outlet reports it, be suspicious.
  • Verify images: Use Google Reverse Image Search or TinEye.
  • Ask who benefits: Who gains from you believing this story?
  • Check your own biases: We tend to believe what confirms what we already think.

Crime and Punishment

Justice or Revenge? Understanding Crime and Punishment in Today's World

From ancient laws to modern prison systems, humanity has always struggled with the same question: what do we do with those who break the rules that hold society together?

JC
ADSO JULIO CÉSAR RUDAS SANCHEZ · September 2026 · 7 min read

Defining Crime and Punishment

A crime is any act that violates the laws of a society and is subject to legal punishment. Punishment is the response that the State applies to those who commit crimes. But the real debate has never been whether to punish — it has always been how and why.

Different societies throughout history have answered these questions very differently. Some have emphasized retribution — making the offender suffer. Others have focused on rehabilitation — helping the offender become a productive member of society again. Most modern systems try to balance both, with varying degrees of success.

"The degree of civilization in a society can be judged by entering its prisons."

— Fyodor Dostoevsky, The House of the Dead

The Four Goals of Punishment

  • Retribution: The offender deserves to suffer proportionally to the harm they caused. This is the most ancient form of justice — "an eye for an eye."
  • Deterrence: The punishment serves as a warning to others. If the consequences are severe enough, people will think twice before committing a crime.
  • Rehabilitation: The goal is to reform the criminal through education, therapy, and reintegration programs so they can return to society as a functioning member.
  • Incapacitation: Keeping dangerous individuals away from society through imprisonment or other restrictions to prevent further harm.

Crime in Colombia — A Personal Perspective

Growing up in Cartagena, crime was not an abstract concept for me. I have seen how economic inequality, lack of opportunities, and inadequate education push young people toward illegal activities. This taught me something important: most crimes have social roots, not just individual moral failures.

In recent years, Colombia has made significant progress in reducing violent crime rates. The homicide rate has dropped substantially compared to the 1990s. However, the prison system remains severely overcrowded, and recidivism — the rate of people returning to crime after release — remains a major challenge that the current system has not been able to resolve.

I believe that simply locking people up without addressing the conditions that led them to crime in the first place is not justice — it is just delay. Real justice requires investment in education, in mental health, in economic opportunity. Prevention is always cheaper and more humane than punishment.

Different Approaches Around the World

  • Norway: Focuses intensely on rehabilitation. Prison conditions are humane. The reoffending rate is below 20% — one of the lowest in the world.
  • United States: Relies heavily on long sentences and incapacitation. Has the highest incarceration rate in the world — over 2 million people imprisoned.
  • Japan: Uses strong community pressure, restorative practices, and a highly efficient court system. Conviction rates exceed 99%.
  • Colombia: A mixed system that has been moving toward restorative justice in recent years, especially for non-violent offenders, with promising but uneven results.

My Reflection

Studying the topic of crime and punishment in English has not only expanded my vocabulary — it has deepened my understanding of justice as a philosophical and social concept. A system that only punishes without trying to understand or correct the root causes of crime will always be incomplete. I believe that true justice heals society — it does not simply make it suffer in return. The countries that have understood this tend to be both safer and more humane. That is not a coincidence.

How Watching Series in English Changed Everything

I started watching Doctor House with Spanish subtitles. Then English subtitles. Then no subtitles at all. I didn't notice when the switch happened.

JC
ADSO - JULIO CÉSAR RUDAS SÁNCHEZ
June 2026 · 3 min read

The accidental language school

I did not set out to use TV as a learning method. I just wanted to watch a good series. But somewhere between Doctor House's first chemistry class and the final season, I realized I was no longer reading the subtitles. I was just watching.

The process was gradual and largely unconscious. Spanish subtitles gave me the story. English subtitles gave me the vocabulary. And eventually, my brain connected the sounds to the meaning without needing a translation in between. That is, I believe, when real language acquisition begins.

My recommended series for learning English

  • Breaking Bad: Complex vocabulary, moral dilemmas, and the clearest dialogue I have ever heard in American English.
  • The Office (US): Everyday conversational English, humor, and workplace vocabulary.
  • Planet Earth (BBC): Formal narration, nature vocabulary, and David Attenborough's perfect diction.
  • Ted Lasso: Positive, motivational English with a mix of British and American accents.
  • Dark (with English dub): Complex time-based vocabulary — challenging but rewarding.

Other hobbies that help me learn

Beyond series, I enjoy cycling while listening to English podcasts — "TED Talks Daily" and "6 Minute English" from the BBC are my favorites. I also contribute occasionally to open-source projects on GitHub, where all communication happens in English, which forces me to write professionally and clearly.

The best language learning happens when you forget you are learning. Find what you love — series, music, sports, games — and find it in English. The vocabulary will follow. The grammar will follow. The confidence will follow. Just start with something you genuinely enjoy.
